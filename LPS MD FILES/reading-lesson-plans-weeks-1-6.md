## Week 1 Content Pack — "The Journey" (Robin Nathan)

**Type:** Fiction | **Reading level:** Year 5/6
**Theme:** Bird migration, courage, growing up
**Curriculum:** NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6)

> **Note:** this was the proof-of-concept template every later week's content pack followed.

---

### Story Snapshot (for you)
Wilfred is a young godwit who doesn't want to join his flock's huge migration from Alaska to New Zealand, led by the respected Captain Thunder. Along the way the flock survives a storm, and Wilfred grows from reluctant and scared into brave, gaining a new understanding of duty and courage. It's a gentle, character-driven opener for the term — light on bicultural content by design, since it's a fictional lead-in to the real, non-fiction kūaka migration story that appears later (Week 8).

---

### 🟦 MONDAY — Whole Class: Lead-in, Vocabulary & Comprehension

#### Lead-in questions
1. Have you ever had to do something you didn't want to, even though everyone told you it was important? How did you feel?
2. Look at the title and first page. Who do you think "Wilfred" is? What might "the Journey" be?
3. Do you know any animals that travel huge distances every year? What do you already know about migration?

#### Key vocabulary
| Word | Kid-friendly meaning |
|---|---|
| godwit | a long-beaked shorebird that flies between Alaska and NZ every year |
| flock | a large group of birds travelling or living together |
| solemnly | in a serious, formal way |
| elder | an older, respected member of a group |
| captain | the leader in charge of a journey or crew |
| duty | something you must do because it is your responsibility |
| exhausted | extremely tired, with no energy left |
| amazement | a feeling of great surprise |
| "too chicken" | idiom — too scared to do something |
| "birdbrain" | joking insult meaning silly (funny here — they ARE birds!) |

#### Basic comprehension questions (literal — whole class)
1. Where do the godwits live in summer, and where are they flying to?
2. About how far is the journey, in kilometres?
3. Why doesn't Wilfred want to go at first?
4. Who is Captain Thunder, and why does everyone respect him?
5. What happened to Flight Officer Wingspan during the storm?
6. How did Captain Thunder and the flock survive the storm?

**Curriculum tags:** oral language • vocabulary knowledge • activating prior knowledge • literal comprehension

---

### 🟩 TUESDAY — Level Groups: Guided Reading

**Group A — needs more support**
- Re-read pages 1–3 together with teacher.
- Task: sequence 4 picture/sentence cards in the order events happened.

**Group B — at level (teacher-led guided group)**
- Focus: how dialogue shows character.
- Task: find 2 pieces of evidence Wilfred is nervous, and 2 that show he's brave by the end.

**Group C — extending**
- Focus: inference & humour — why is calling a bird "chicken" or "birdbrain" funny?
- Task: write one more line of dialogue Wilfred might say once they land in NZ, staying true to character.

**Curriculum tags:** reading progression • fluency & comprehension at instructional level • differentiated instruction

---

### 🟨 WEDNESDAY — Pairs: Reading Activities

1. **Hot Seat: Interview Captain Thunder** — One partner is Captain Thunder, the other a young godwit reporter. Ask about the storm, Wingspan, and why the Journey matters — then swap.
2. **Storm Sequencing** — Cut up 6 sentences describing the storm scene. Pairs order them and explain how they knew.
3. **Vocabulary Match-Up** — Card game matching each vocab word to its kid-friendly meaning — fastest correct pair wins a point.
4. **True or False Detective** — Given 8 statements about the story, pairs find evidence in the text to prove or disprove each one.

**Curriculum tags:** comprehension strategies (inference, evidence-finding) • collaborative talk

---

### 🟧 THURSDAY / 🟥 FRIDAY — Understanding Project

#### This week's project options (pairs choose one)
- **Comic strip** — retell the storm scene from Captain Thunder's memory, in 6 panels.
- **"Would you go?" persuasive mini-speech** — argue whether you'd join the Journey if you were Wilfred.
- **Fact file / travel brochure** — "Visit New Zealand — Godwit Summer Home!"
- **Podcast-style recording** — in role as Wilfred, record a "voice diary" the night before leaving Alaska.

#### Thursday (build/create time)
- ~5 min: recap the story + choose project format
- ~35 min: plan and build/draft
- ~5–10 min: teacher check-in per pair

#### Friday (finish + present)
- ~20–25 min: finish and polish
- ~20–25 min: presentations (2–3 min per pair) + peer feedback ("2 stars and a wish")

**Curriculum tags:** oral language — presenting ideas clearly • key competencies — participating & contributing, relating to others • synthesis — creating a text in response to reading

---

### Christian Reflection — School Values
*Soli Deo Gloria — To God Alone the Glory*

| Value | Reflection question |
|---|---|
| **Aroha** (Love) | Wilfred's family is patient and encouraging even when he grumbles and doesn't want to go. How can we show aroha to someone in our own family or class who is scared or reluctant, the way Wilfred was? |
| **Mōhio** (Know) | Godwits know exactly when and where to go because that's how God designed them. What does this remind us about trusting God's plan for our lives, even when we can't see the whole journey ahead? |
| **Manaaki** (Serve) | Captain Thunder used his courage and experience to protect and guide the whole flock. What gift or talent has God given you that you could use to serve others this week, even when it feels hard? |
| **Pānga** (Impact) | The godwits' journey brings new life to two different lands, every single year. How can facing a challenge we don't want to face still end up bringing good to others around us? |

## Week 2 Content Pack — "Journey of a Waka" (School Journal)

**Type:** Non-fiction (School Journal) | **Reading level:** Year 5/6
**Theme:** Voyaging, navigation, wayfinding
**Curriculum:** NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6) — with strong bicultural links

---

### Story Snapshot (for you)
A multi-section, non-fiction explainer on how the ancestors of Māori sailed to Aotearoa aboard waka hourua (double-hulled voyaging canoes) — covering the signs that told them it was time to leave, the essential items they carried, the four key crew roles (rangatira, tohunga, kaihautū, kaiurungi), and the signs that told them land was near. Strong bicultural content — real oral histories and voyaging knowledge, treated with genuine respect throughout.

---

### 🟦 MONDAY — Whole Class: Lead-in, Vocabulary & Comprehension

#### Lead-in questions
1. If you were about to sail across the world's biggest ocean with no map and no GPS, what would you pack? What would you need to know?
2. What clues in nature might tell you a storm is coming, or that land is near?
3. Have you heard of waka hourua, or Polynesian wayfinding/navigation? What do you already know?

#### Key vocabulary
| Word | Kid-friendly meaning |
|---|---|
| waka hourua | a large double-hulled voyaging canoe used for long ocean journeys |
| rangatira | the captain/leader of the waka |
| tohunga | the priest and navigator — reads the sky, stars and sea |
| kaihautū | the person who directs the paddlers |
| kaiurungi | the steerer(s) of the waka |
| adze | a tool for shaping wood, like a curved axe |
| mana | prestige, authority, and respect |
| oral histories | stories and knowledge passed down by speaking, generation to generation |
| archaeology | the study of the past through objects and sites people left behind |
| migrating | travelling regularly between two places, often with the seasons |

#### Basic comprehension questions (literal — whole class)
1. Where did the ancestors of Māori sail from, and roughly how long ago did they begin arriving in Aotearoa?
2. Name three signs that told the ancestors it was the right time to leave.
3. Name three essential items carried on the waka.
4. What animals may have helped guide the waka on their journeys?
5. Name three signs that told the ancestors land was near.
6. What were the four main roles on a waka hourua, and what did each person do?

**Curriculum tags:** oral language • vocabulary knowledge (incl. kupu Māori) • non-fiction text features • literal comprehension • bicultural perspectives

---

### 🟩 TUESDAY — Level Groups: Guided Reading

**Group A — needs more support**
- Re-read one section at a time together (e.g. "Navigation," then "Essential items").
- Task: highlight or underline one key fact from each section read.

**Group B — at level (teacher-led guided group)**
- Focus: locating information across a non-linear, multi-section text.
- Task: answer 3–4 questions requiring info from different sections; match each role name to its job.

**Group C — extending**
- Focus: comparing traditional and modern navigation.
- Task: compare wayfinding (stars, currents, wind, birds) with modern GPS/compass. Which would you trust more, and why?

**Curriculum tags:** reading progression — using text features to locate information • differentiated instruction • bicultural perspectives

---

### 🟨 WEDNESDAY — Pairs: Reading Activities

1. **Text Feature Scavenger Hunt** — Pairs find and list every heading/section title, and explain what each section is about in their own words.
2. **Role Card Match** — Match cards with role titles (rangatira, tohunga, kaihautū, kaiurungi) to a card describing that job — race another pair.
3. **Packing List Debate** — Given the "essential items" list, pairs rank the top 5 most important for survival and justify their order.
4. **Nature-Clue Detectives** — Sort mixed clue cards into: Signs it's time to leave / Signs land is near / Navigation method.

**Curriculum tags:** non-fiction text features • comprehension strategies (locating & categorising information) • collaborative talk • bicultural perspectives

---

### 🟧 THURSDAY / 🟥 FRIDAY — Understanding Project

#### This week's project options (pairs choose one)
- **Design your own infographic** — "How to Survive a Waka Hourua Journey" — in the same labelled-section style as the original.
- **Role-play news report** — interview a tohunga about how they read the stars, currents and wildlife to find their way.
- **Labelled diagram/model** — a waka hourua showing where key supplies, food, water and people would be positioned.
- **Persuasive pitch** — "Why our waka should carry [an animal] on board" using evidence about kurī or kiore.

#### Thursday (build/create time)
- ~5 min: recap the text + choose project format
- ~35 min: plan and build/draft
- ~5–10 min: teacher check-in per pair

#### Friday (finish + present)
- ~20–25 min: finish and polish
- ~20–25 min: presentations (2–3 min per pair) + peer feedback ("2 stars and a wish")

**Curriculum tags:** oral language — presenting ideas clearly • key competencies — participating & contributing, relating to others • synthesis — creating a text in response to reading • bicultural perspectives

---

### Christian Reflection — School Values
*Soli Deo Gloria — To God Alone the Glory*

| Value | Reflection question |
|---|---|
| **Aroha** (Love) | The waka carried whole families and communities together, all depending on each other to survive the journey. What does this show us about what it means to love and care for others as part of a team? |
| **Mōhio** (Know) | The ancestors used the stars, currents and signs in creation to find their way across the ocean. How is this similar to how we can look to God's creation and His Word to help guide our own lives? |
| **Manaaki** (Serve) | Every person on the waka had a role — navigator, steerer, paddler — and used their own skill to serve the whole crew. What's one gift God has given you that you could use to serve your class or family this week? |
| **Pānga** (Impact) | The voyage of the ancestors led to the beginning of a whole new nation. How can the choices we make now create a positive impact that reaches further than just ourselves? |

## Week 3 Content Pack — "Beyond the Reef" (Moriori oral history)

**Type:** Non-fiction (Moriori oral history) | **Reading level:** Year 5/6
**Theme:** Migration to Rēkohu
**Curriculum:** NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6) — with strong bicultural links

---

### Story Snapshot (for you)
Ri'i's village is preparing to secretly leave their home at night, after fighting has broken out and Ri'i's grandfather Horopapa — grieving the loss of his daughter — dreams of a peaceful new island. Ri'i is entrusted with a secret job to help prepare for departure, and has to keep it hidden even from his close friend Moe. The story follows the emotional lead-up to departure, the farewell, and the moment the canoes finally cross beyond the reef. This is a genuine Moriori oral history handed down for generations — treat its grief and hope with the same respect given to any real historical account.

---

### 🟦 MONDAY — Whole Class: Lead-in, Vocabulary & Comprehension

#### Lead-in questions
1. Have you ever had to keep a big secret from a close friend — one you knew might hurt them? How did it feel?
2. Imagine leaving your home forever, at night, not knowing if you'd ever return. What would be running through your mind?
3. What do you already know about the Moriori people and Rēkohu (the Chatham Islands)?

#### Key vocabulary
| Word | Kid-friendly meaning |
|---|---|
| paihihi | canoes (as glossed in the text) |
| karakii | a chant/incantation, said for strength or a safe journey |
| umere | chants, part of the departure ritual |
| hunau | family |
| etchu | spiritual beings |
| ominous | making you feel something bad is about to happen |
| ritual | a set of actions done the same way, often for a special or spiritual reason |
| secrecy | the state of keeping something hidden |
| navigator | the person who works out the direction and course of a journey |
| hull | the main body of a boat or canoe |

#### Basic comprehension questions (literal — whole class)
1. Who is Ri'i, and why is his village leaving Rēkohu?
2. What does Horopapa dream about, and why does it matter to him?
3. What was Ri'i's secret job in the weeks before departure?
4. Why couldn't Moe's family know about the departure plan?
5. What does Horopapa give Ri'i just before the canoes leave (the karakii)?
6. What happens as the canoes finally cross beyond the reef?

**Curriculum tags:** oral language • vocabulary knowledge (incl. Moriori/te reo terms) • literal comprehension • bicultural perspectives (handled sensitively)

---

### 🟩 TUESDAY — Level Groups: Guided Reading

**Group A — needs more support**
- Re-read the three "•••" sections together with the teacher.
- Task: sequence 6 event cards in order, from the fighting breaking out to the canoes crossing the reef.

**Group B — at level (teacher-led guided group)**
- Focus: understanding mixed emotions.
- Task: find evidence Ri'i feels both fear and hope; discuss why the departure needed to stay secret.

**Group C — extending**
- Focus: inference & cross-text connection.
- Task: what might Horopapa's dream represent? Compare the roles/preparations here with *Journey of a Waka* (Week 2) — what's the same, what's different?

**Curriculum tags:** reading progression — inference, understanding time-shifts • differentiated instruction • bicultural perspectives

---

### 🟨 WEDNESDAY — Pairs: Reading Activities

1. **Farewell Hot Seat** — One partner is Ri'i, the other Moe, at their last meeting by the reef. Re-enact or extend their conversation, staying true to how each character feels.
2. **Event Sequencing** — Pairs order 6–8 cut-up sentences describing key moments and justify using text evidence.
3. **Vocabulary Match-Up** — Match Moriori/te reo terms (paihihi, karakii, umere, hunau, etchu) and other vocab to their meanings.
4. **Secret-Keeper Dilemma** — Pairs discuss: was it right to keep the plan secret from Moe? Each argues one side, then write a joint conclusion.

**Curriculum tags:** comprehension strategies (inference, evidence-finding) • collaborative talk • bicultural perspectives

---

### 🟧 THURSDAY / 🟥 FRIDAY — Understanding Project

#### This week's project options (pairs choose one)
- **Comic strip** — retell the farewell scene between Ri'i and Moe, in 6 panels.
- **Diary entry** — write as Ri'i, the night before leaving, describing his fears and hopes.
- **Persuasive mini-speech** — should the village council have listened to "the way of no more fighting" sooner?
- **Labelled voyage map** — draw and label the four canoes and their crew roles, linking back to *Journey of a Waka*.

#### Thursday (build/create time)
- ~5 min: recap the story + choose project format
- ~35 min: plan and build/draft
- ~5–10 min: teacher check-in per pair

#### Friday (finish + present)
- ~20–25 min: finish and polish
- ~20–25 min: presentations (2–3 min per pair) + peer feedback ("2 stars and a wish")

**Curriculum tags:** oral language — presenting ideas clearly • key competencies — participating & contributing, relating to others • synthesis — creating a text in response to reading • bicultural perspectives

---

### Christian Reflection — School Values
*Soli Deo Gloria — To God Alone the Glory*

| Value | Reflection question |
|---|---|
| **Aroha** (Love) | Even while hurting after his daughter's death, Horopapa chooses hope and peace instead of holding onto anger. How can we choose love over anger when we've been badly hurt by someone? |
| **Mōhio** (Know) | Horopapa holds onto the memory of a peaceful time and dreams of a better future for his people. How does knowing God's story help us hold onto hope even when things around us feel hard or uncertain? |
| **Manaaki** (Serve) | Ri'i is given an important job to help his people, even though he is still young. How can we serve faithfully with what we have, even when we feel too young, small, or unready? |
| **Pānga** (Impact) | The families' journey is meant to bring a new, peaceful way of life to a new land. What's one way you could help bring more peace into our own school or home this week? |

## Week 4 Content Pack — "A Short History of Rēkohu"

**Type:** Non-fiction | **Reading level:** Year 5/6
**Theme:** Moriori history, place
**Curriculum:** NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6) — Aotearoa histories

---

### Story Snapshot (for you)
A timeline-structured non-fiction text tracing Moriori history from the Law of Nunuku (an agreement that ended warfare between Moriori tribes) through the 1835 invasion by Ngāti Mutunga and Ngāti Tama, the false story published about Moriori in a 1916 School Journal, the significance of Tame Horomona Rehe (Tommy Solomon), and up to today's efforts to recognise and support Moriori people and culture. This week is a serious, direct link to Aotearoa's histories — real injustice and real resilience — and should be taught with the same care given to any true account of harm done to real people.

---

### 🟦 MONDAY — Whole Class: Lead-in, Vocabulary & Comprehension

#### Lead-in questions
1. Is it easy or hard to choose peace, even when you're being treated unfairly? Why?
2. Have you heard of the Moriori people or the Chatham Islands before today?
3. Why do you think it matters to learn the true history of a place and its people?

#### Key vocabulary
| Word | Kid-friendly meaning |
|---|---|
| tchakat henu | "people of the land" — how Moriori describe themselves |
| Law of Nunuku | Nunuku-whenua's agreement that ended warfare between Moriori tribes forever |
| invasion | when a group of people forcefully takes over land that belongs to others |
| slavery | forcing people to work for others against their will, with no freedom |
| dendroglyphs | carvings made into living trees — a form of Moriori art and record |
| marae | a communal meeting ground, central to cultural life |
| Treaty settlement | an agreement to address past injustice between government and iwi/group |
| resilience | the ability to keep going and recover, even after very hard times |
| census | an official count of a country's population |
| tribunal | a type of court that hears and rules on claims |

#### Basic comprehension questions (literal — whole class)
1. What does "Rēkohu" mean, and where is it located?
2. What is the Law of Nunuku, and who created it?
3. What happened when Ngāti Mutunga and Ngāti Tama arrived on Rēkohu in 1835?
4. Why was the story published in the 1916 School Journal untrue, and why did it matter?
5. Who was Tame Horomona Rehe (Tommy Solomon), and why is he significant?
6. Name two things being done today to recognise and support Moriori people and culture.

**Curriculum tags:** oral language • vocabulary knowledge • non-fiction timeline text features • literal comprehension • bicultural/Aotearoa histories perspectives (handled with care)

---

### 🟩 TUESDAY — Level Groups: Guided Reading

**Group A — needs more support**
- Re-read the timeline in date order with the teacher, from the Law of Nunuku through to 1900.
- Task: with support, sequence 5–6 major event cards in order using the dates given.

**Group B — at level (teacher-led guided group)**
- Focus: explaining significance in own words.
- Task: explain what the Law of Nunuku says and why it mattered; find evidence of what happened in 1835 and how Moriori responded.

**Group C — extending**
- Focus: critical thinking about historical narratives.
- Task: why does the false 1916 story "have a lasting impact"? Compare that to today's efforts to reclaim the true history.

**Curriculum tags:** reading progression — chronological/timeline features, cause and effect • differentiated instruction • bicultural/Aotearoa histories perspectives

---

### 🟨 WEDNESDAY — Pairs: Reading Activities

1. **Timeline Sequencing** — Pairs match event cards to their correct dates/order.
2. **Vocabulary Match-Up** — Match key terms (tchakat henu, Law of Nunuku, dendroglyphs, marae, resilience) to their meanings.
3. **Law of Nunuku Discussion** — Discuss respectfully: what does it mean to choose peace even when treated unfairly? Write a joint response.
4. **Fact-Check Detectives** — Compare the false 1916 School Journal excerpt with the real history told elsewhere in the text.

**Curriculum tags:** non-fiction text features • comprehension strategies (evidence-finding, comparing sources) • collaborative talk • bicultural/Aotearoa histories perspectives

---

### 🟧 THURSDAY / 🟥 FRIDAY — Understanding Project

#### This week's project options (pairs choose one)

- **Persuasive speech** — "Why is it important to learn true history, even when it's hard?"
- **Fact file** — research Kopinga Marae or the Chatham Island black robin conservation story.
- **Respectful role-play interview** — in role as a Moriori elder today, discuss the meaning of the Law of Nunuku.

#### Thursday (build/create time)
- ~5 min: recap the timeline + choose project format
- ~35 min: plan and build/draft
- ~5–10 min: teacher check-in per pair

#### Friday (finish + present)
- ~20–25 min: finish and polish
- ~20–25 min: presentations (2–3 min per pair) + peer feedback ("2 stars and a wish")

**Curriculum tags:** oral language — presenting ideas clearly • key competencies — participating & contributing, relating to others, thinking critically • bicultural/Aotearoa histories perspectives

---

## Week 5 Content Pack — "Where No Boat Could Live"

**Type:** Non-fiction | **Reading level:** Year 5/6
**Theme:** Chatham Islands, weather/seafaring
**Curriculum:** NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6) — with strong Science/Technology link

---

### Story Snapshot (for you)
An explanation text on how Moriori engineered the wash-through waka — a design adapted for the "roaring forties," the fierce seas around the Chatham Islands — after their original dugout canoes proved unsuited to the conditions. The text includes a 1791 quote from a European visitor, James Johnstone, who dismissively compared the waka to "wheelbarrows," and then explains the real engineering genius behind the design. Continues the term's throughline of correcting outsiders' biased first impressions of Moriori ingenuity.

---

### 🟦 MONDAY — Whole Class: Lead-in, Vocabulary & Comprehension

#### Lead-in questions
1. If you had to build a boat to survive huge, stormy seas, what would you want it to do — float no matter what? Never sink? Carry a lot?
2. Have you ever made something clever out of natural materials (sticks, reeds, plants)? What did you make, and how did you solve a problem with it?
3. What do you know about the Chatham Islands and their weather?

#### Key vocabulary
| Word | Kid-friendly meaning |
|---|---|
| roaring forties | a stretch of ocean known for very strong winds and storms |
| dugout canoe | a canoe carved from a single hollowed-out log |
| wash-through waka | a boat built to let water flow through it, rather than keep it out |
| ballast | something heavy that makes a boat stable |
| buoyant | able to float |
| capsize | to overturn in the water |
| bow | the front of a boat |
| stern | the back of a boat |
| hull | the bottom/body of a boat |
| innovators | people who invent new and clever solutions to problems |

#### Basic comprehension questions (literal — whole class)
1. Why are the waters around the Chatham Islands nicknamed "the roaring forties"?
2. Why didn't the Moriori's original dugout canoes work well in Rēkohu's seas?
3. What were the two types of wash-through waka, and how were they different in size?
4. What materials were used to build a wash-through waka?
5. Why didn't the wash-through waka need to be waterproof?
6. What did European visitors think of the waka at first, and why were they wrong?

**Curriculum tags:** oral language • vocabulary knowledge (nautical/technical terms) • literal comprehension • bicultural perspectives • cross-curricular Science/Technology link

---

### 🟩 TUESDAY — Level Groups: Guided Reading

**Group A — needs more support**
- Re-read the sections on waka kōrari and waka pahī with the teacher.
- Task: label a diagram of a wash-through waka with its key parts (bow, stern, hull, runners).

**Group B — at level (teacher-led guided group)**
- Focus: explaining how and why the design worked.
- Task: explain why letting water "wash through" made the waka safer; find evidence of how Europeans misunderstood the design.

**Group C — extending**
- Focus: evaluating bias in a primary source.
- Task: compare Johnstone's 1791 "wheelbarrows" quote with what we now know. Why might a visitor misjudge something they don't understand? Connect to Week 4's false 1916 story.

**Curriculum tags:** reading progression — technical vocabulary, evaluating primary sources • differentiated instruction • bicultural perspectives

## Week 6 Content Pack — "Te Pokohiwi-o-Kupe: Aotearoa's First Settlement" (interview)

**Type:** Non-fiction (interview) | **Reading level:** Year 6
**Theme:** Wairau Bar archaeology
**Curriculum:** NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6) — with strong Science and Aotearoa histories links

---

### Story Snapshot (for you)
An interview text exploring the archaeological discoveries at Te Pokohiwi-o-Kupe (Wairau Bar), Aotearoa's earliest known settlement site — covering how scientists used DNA, radiocarbon dating, and evidence like a tropical shell chisel to piece together where the first settlers came from and how they lived. The text also covers the disrespectful removal of Rangitāne ancestral remains (kōiwi tangata) in the 1940s–50s, and the years-long fight by kaumātua Hohua Peter MacDonald and others for their return (repatriation). A serious, real account of harm and its resolution — teach with real weight given to both the injustice and the hopeful outcome.

---

### 🟦 MONDAY — Whole Class: Lead-in, Vocabulary & Comprehension

#### Lead-in questions
1. If archaeologists dug up your backyard in 700 years, what might they learn about you just from what you'd thrown away?
2. How do you think scientists can figure out how old something is, or even where a person was born, just from bones and teeth?
3. Why do you think it matters how we treat ancestral remains and burial sites?

#### Key vocabulary
| Word | Kid-friendly meaning |
|---|---|
| tūpuna | ancestors |
| kōiwi tangata | ancestral (human) remains |
| wāhi tapu | a sacred place |
| midden | an ancient rubbish dump full of discarded bones, shells and tools |
| radiocarbon dating | a scientific method used to estimate the age of things like bone or shell |
| DNA | the genetic information in a cell that tells us about ancestry |
| argillite (pakohe) | a hard stone, found only at the top of the South Island, used to make tools |
| taonga | treasured cultural objects |
| repatriation | returning ancestral remains or cultural objects to their community |
| hāngī pits | pits used for traditional cooking, often for large feasts |

#### Basic comprehension questions (literal — whole class)
1. What is Te Pokohiwi-o-Kupe also known as, and where is it located?
2. About how long ago was Te Pokohiwi first settled, and how do scientists know this?
3. What evidence suggests some of the people buried there were born outside Aotearoa?
4. Why was Te Pokohiwi a good place to settle?
5. What happened to the burial sites and taonga at Te Pokohiwi in the 1940s–50s?
6. What is being done today to address this, and who is leading it?

**Curriculum tags:** oral language • vocabulary knowledge • non-fiction interview text features • literal comprehension • bicultural/Aotearoa histories perspectives • cross-curricular Science link
