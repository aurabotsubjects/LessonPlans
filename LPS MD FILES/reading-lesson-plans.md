### 2. The Weekly Rhythm (Mon–Fri, same shape every week)

One story = one week. Same 4-day skeleton every time so it becomes routine for students, freeing up their energy for the actual reading rather than "what do we do today."

| Day | Focus | Grouping | Curriculum emphasis |
|-----|-------|----------|---------------------|
| **Monday** | Lead-in + whole-class vocab & comprehension | Whole class | Oral language, vocabulary knowledge, activating prior knowledge |
| **Tuesday** | Guided reading | Level groups | Reading progression — decoding, fluency, comprehension at instructional level |
| **Wednesday** | Reading activities | Pairs | Comprehension strategies, collaborative talk, practice & knowledge |
| **Thursday** | Understanding project (part 1) | Pairs/small groups | Deeper comprehension, synthesis, creating a text/response |
| **Friday** | Understanding project (part 2) + Presentations | Pairs/small groups → whole class | Oral language, presenting ideas, key competencies (relating to others, participating & contributing) |

**Every lesson ≈ 50 minutes.** Suggested internal timing (adjustable per lesson type in the app):
- 5 min: settle + recap/hook
- 35–40 min: main activity
- 5–10 min: share-back / exit check

### 3. Day-by-Day Detail

#### 🟦 Monday — Whole Class: Lead-in, Vocabulary & Comprehension
**Purpose:** Build the shared foundation everyone needs before independent/group work starts.

- **Lead-in question(s)** — 2–3 hook questions per story (predicting from title/cover image, connecting to students' own experience, activating background knowledge — e.g. "Have you ever moved somewhere new? What would you take?")
- **Vocabulary breakdown** — pull 6–10 key/tricky words from the story; explicit teaching (meaning, syllables, example sentence, connect to kupu Māori where relevant)
- **First read / read-aloud** — teacher reads or plays audio of the whole text; whole-class basic comprehension check (literal questions — who/what/where/when)
- **Curriculum link:** oral language component of structured literacy; vocabulary knowledge; activating prior knowledge as a comprehension strategy (NZC English Phase 2, Years 4–6)

#### 🟩 Tuesday — Level Groups: Guided Reading
**Purpose:** Reading practice matched to instructional level, teacher rotates or runs one group at a time.

- Groups re-read the text (or a levelled excerpt) at their own pace/level
- Teacher-led group gets explicit decoding/fluency/comprehension instruction
- Other groups: independent re-read + simple task (e.g. sequence the events, find evidence for an answer)
- **Curriculum link:** differentiated instruction matched to reading progression; fluency and comprehension strategies at instructional level

#### 🟨 Wednesday — Pairs: Reading Activities
**Purpose:** Active, hands-on comprehension work — this is where "fun" lives.

- Paired/hands-on activities such as: sequencing cards, vocabulary matching games, "hot seat" character/interviewee questioning, comprehension scavenger hunt, draw-and-label diagrams, true/false detective (find evidence in text), question each other using question cubes/dice
- **Curriculum link:** collaborative talk, comprehension strategies (inference, evidence-finding), practice consolidation

#### 🟧 Thursday — Understanding Project (Part 1)
**Purpose:** Students synthesise their understanding into something they create — start the project.

- Pairs/small groups choose or are assigned a project format (rotate weekly for variety — see menu below)
- Planning + drafting/building time
- Teacher checks in with each group

#### 🟥 Friday — Understanding Project (Part 2) + Presentations
**Purpose:** Finish, polish, and share.

- First ~20–25 min: finish project
- Remaining time: presentations to class (2–3 min per group) + peer feedback (e.g. 2 stars and a wish, thumbs poll)
- **Curriculum link:** oral language — presenting ideas clearly; key competencies — participating & contributing, relating to others

**Understanding Project menu (rotate through the term so it stays fresh):**
- Comic strip / storyboard retelling
- Diorama or model of a key scene/setting
- Podcast-style recorded interview (in role)
- Poster/infographic (non-fiction texts — facts, timeline, diagram)
- Short dramatic re-enactment / freeze-frame scenes
- "Fact file" or travel brochure (great fit for the voyaging/migration texts)
- Book trailer / pitch to sell the story to another class
- Debate or "would you have gone?" persuasive mini-speech

## Week 7 Content Pack — "The Long Pause" (Connected, Level 3, by Dr Amber Aranui)

**Type:** Non-fiction (explanation text, multiple competing scientific theories) | **Reading level:** Year 6 (challenge text)
**Theme:** The 1,000-year gap between the Lapita migration and the settlement of East Polynesia/Aotearoa
**Curriculum:** NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6) — with strong Science (archaeology, hypothesis-testing) links

---

### Story Snapshot (for you)
About 3,500 years ago, the Lapita people rapidly explored and settled West Polynesia (Fiji, Sāmoa, Tonga) in just 500 years. Then — mysteriously — exploration stopped for at least another 1,000 years, before people finally voyaged on to Hawai'i, Rapa Nui, and Aotearoa. This text presents **three competing scientific theories** for why: (1) canoe technology wasn't yet good enough for the longer, rougher East Polynesian crossings, (2) resource competition and possible droughts in West Polynesia may have pushed people to search for new land, and (3) computer models suggest wind patterns changed at just the right times to make eastward sailing possible. This is the most complex text of the term — a great one for teaching how scientists evaluate multiple, competing explanations rather than settling for a single simple answer.

**Nice link back to earlier weeks:** radiocarbon dating (Week 6), and waka technology/the waka hourua (Weeks 2, 3 and 5) all reappear here — worth pointing out explicitly.

---

### 🟦 MONDAY — Whole Class: Lead-in, Vocabulary & Comprehension

#### Lead-in questions
1. Imagine sailing into the ocean with no idea what's on the other side, or if there's anything there at all. What would make you brave enough to go?
2. Why do you think people might stop exploring somewhere for a very long time, then suddenly start again?
3. What do you already know about how the Pacific Islands, including Aotearoa, were first settled?

#### Key vocabulary
| Word | Kid-friendly meaning |
|---|---|
| Lapita | the early Pacific people who first migrated from the Bismarck Islands out into the Pacific |
| migration | the movement of people from one place to settle in another |
| archaeologist | a scientist who studies the past through objects and remains people left behind |
| hypothesis | a possible explanation that still needs more evidence to prove it |
| outrigger canoe | a canoe with a float attached to one side for balance, but limited in size and distance |
| waka hourua | a double-hulled canoe that could travel further and carry more |
| drought | a long period of very dry weather |
| computer models | computer programs that use maths and data to predict what might have happened |
| hospitable | pleasant and favourable for living in |
| radiocarbon dating | measuring radioactive carbon in remains to estimate how long ago something lived |

#### Whole-class read
- This is a denser text with multiple theories — read section by section, pausing after each theory to summarise it in one sentence as a class.

#### Basic comprehension questions (literal — whole class)
1. Who were the Lapita, and where did they first settle?
2. About how long was "the long pause" between the two migrations?
3. Name two possible reasons scientists suggest for the long pause.
4. What is the difference between an outrigger canoe and a waka hourua?
5. How do computer models help researchers understand wind patterns from the past?
6. Which was the last major area of the Pacific to be settled?

**Curriculum tags:** oral language • vocabulary knowledge • literal comprehension • bicultural/Aotearoa perspectives • cross-curricular Science link (hypothesis-testing, radiocarbon dating)

---

**Group A — needs more support**

**Group B — at level (teacher-led guided group)**

**Group C — extending**
- Focus: evaluating multiple competing explanations.
- Task: compare all three theories. Which do you find most convincing, and why? Discuss why scientists test several hypotheses rather than settling for one simple answer.

**Curriculum tags:** reading progression — evaluating multiple explanations, technical vocabulary • differentiated instruction • bicultural perspectives

## Week 8 Content Pack — "Kūaka: The Marathon Bird" (School Journal)

**Type:** Non-fiction (illustrated magazine-style article) | **Reading level:** Year 5/6
**Theme:** The bar-tailed godwit's incredible non-stop migration, and both scientific and traditional Māori ways of understanding it
**Curriculum:** NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6) — with strong Science and bicultural links

---

### Story Snapshot (for you)
Kūaka (bar-tailed godwits) make one of the most extraordinary journeys in the animal kingdom — flying non-stop between Alaska and New Zealand, a distance of around 11,000 km, without landing to rest, eat, or drink. Before departure their bodies transform: they fatten up dramatically and some of their internal organs shrink to save weight, then regrow once they arrive. Modern satellite tracking has revealed the details of this journey scientifically, while Māori traditional knowledge (maramataka, the lunar calendar, and whakataukī, traditional proverbs) has understood and marked the kūaka's arrival and departure as a seasonal signal for many generations. This is a great, uplifting text after some heavier weeks — genuine wonder at what creation can do, alongside real science.

**Nice link back to earlier weeks:** this connects naturally to Week 1 (the fictional godwit story, *The Journey*) and continues the term's Science/bicultural threads from Weeks 6 and 7.

---

### 🟦 MONDAY — Whole Class: Lead-in, Vocabulary & Comprehension

#### Lead-in questions
1. Could you run for 8–9 days without stopping to eat, drink, or rest? How do you think a bird could fly that long, non-stop?
2. What signs in nature (birds arriving, plants flowering) tell people that a new season is coming?
3. What do you already know about the kūaka/godwit and its incredible journey? (A nice link back to Week 1's story, *The Journey*.)

#### Key vocabulary
| Word | Kid-friendly meaning |
|---|---|
| kūaka | the Māori name for the bar-tailed godwit, a large migratory shorebird |
| migratory | travelling regularly between two places, usually with the seasons |
| non-stop | without any pause or rest along the way |
| satellite tracking | using satellites to follow and record an animal's exact movements |
| estuary | where a river meets the sea, often rich in food for wading birds |
| maramataka | the traditional Māori lunar calendar |
| whakataukī | a Māori proverb or saying that carries traditional wisdom |
| V formation | a flying pattern where birds take turns leading to save energy |
| fuel up / fatten up | eating heavily to store enough energy for a long journey |
| organs | internal body parts (like the stomach or liver) that carry out life functions |

#### Whole-class read
- Read the article together, pausing at photos/diagrams of the migration route and body changes.

#### Basic comprehension questions (literal — whole class)
1. What is a kūaka, and where does it migrate between?
2. About how far do kūaka fly, and how long does the journey take?
3. What happens to a kūaka's body before it begins its migration?
4. How do scientists track where kūaka go?
5. What role does the kūaka play in traditional Māori knowledge of the seasons?
6. Why do kūaka fly in a V formation?

**Curriculum tags:** oral language • vocabulary knowledge • literal comprehension • bicultural perspectives (maramataka, whakataukī) • cross-curricular Science link (adaptation, migration, tracking technology)

## Week 9 Content Pack — "Te Rā: The Last of Its Kind" (Connected 2024, an interview by Hamish Ansley)

**Type:** Non-fiction (interview/Q&A format) | **Reading level:** Year 5/6
**Theme:** Te Rā, an extraordinary 200+ year old Māori woven sail — mātauranga Māori, science, and coming home
**Curriculum:** NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6) — with strong Science and bicultural links

---

### Story Snapshot (for you)
Te Rā is probably the only Māori woven sail of its kind left in the world — made from harakeke (flax) in Aotearoa in the 1700s, and kept for most of the last 200 years in the British Museum in London, without anyone quite knowing how it got there. In 2019, three researchers — a weaver/scholar, an expert weaver, and a materials scientist — travelled to London to study Te Rā, combining traditional Māori weaving knowledge (mātauranga) with modern science (like polarised light microscopy) to understand how it was made and what it reveals about Māori voyaging technology. The interview also covers the emotional experience of studying and briefly bringing home a taonga that had been away from its people for so long.

**Nice link back to earlier weeks:** this continues the waka/sail technology thread from Weeks 2, 3, 5 and 7, and echoes Week 6's story of research, evidence, and the significance of bringing a taonga home.

---

### 🟦 MONDAY — Whole Class: Lead-in, Vocabulary & Comprehension

#### Lead-in questions
1. If your family owned something really old and important, and it ended up far away in another country's museum for 200 years, how would you feel about it coming home?
2. What is something in your own family or culture that is treasured and passed down? Why does it matter?
3. Have you ever used both traditional knowledge (like a family recipe or skill) and modern science or tools together to understand or check something?

#### Key vocabulary
| Word | Kid-friendly meaning |
|---|---|
| taonga | a treasured object of cultural, spiritual, or historical significance |
| mātauranga | traditional Māori knowledge |
| whakapapa | genealogy — the history and lineage of a person, family, or object |
| harakeke | the Māori word for flax, a plant used for weaving |
| raranga | the art of weaving |
| muka | fibre prepared from flax, used for cord or binding |
| wānanga | a gathering for learning and discussion |
| mauri | the spirit or life-force of a person, place, or object |
| polarised light microscope | a scientific tool used to examine fine details of materials like fibres |
| tūpuna | ancestors |

#### Whole-class read
- Read as an interview, with different readers taking the parts of Donna, Rānui, and Catherine.

#### Basic comprehension questions (literal — whole class)
1. What is Te Rā, and how old is it?
2. Where was Te Rā kept for most of the last 200 years?
3. What did the three researchers want to find out about Te Rā?
4. What scientific tool did they use, and what did it prove?

## Week 11 Content Pack — "Taki Tahi | Over One, Under One" (Connected 2024, a poem by Briar Grace-Smith)

**Type:** Poetry | **Reading level:** Year 5/6
**Theme:** Weaving a waka's sail together — the rhythm of shared, skilled work, and the sail's first test at sea
**Curriculum:** NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6)

> **A note on this text:** this is a poem, so in line with copyright practice, this pack describes its content, structure and devices rather than quoting lines directly. Students will read the actual poem from their own copy of the text — the activities below ask them to find and discuss specific lines themselves.

---

### Story Snapshot (for you)
This poem describes a group of weavers making a waka's sail from harakeke, working through the "taki tahi" pattern — over one, under one — a phrase that repeats through the poem like the rhythm of the weaving itself. While their hands work, the weavers chat about everyday things (sport, television) right alongside traditional stories and atua (gods) — showing that this skilled, sacred work is also warm and ordinary, done side by side. The poem follows the sail from its finishing, through a quiet moment of pride, to the moment it's finally raised and tested against the wind — and passes the test, powering the waka out across the sea.

**Term capstone:** this is the third and final text from the same journal collection as Weeks 9 and 10 — *Te Rā* (a real interview about an ancient sail) and *Tahu and the Dancing Feathers* (a fictional story about a sail being made). All three texts explore weaving and waka sails through a different genre: non-fiction, fiction, and poetry. This is a great week to draw the whole collection — and arguably the whole term's voyaging theme — together.
